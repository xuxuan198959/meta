### Interview 2: Critical Thinking & Analytical

**What to Expect**

This 45-minute interview explores how you approach problems, work through
ambiguity, and make decisions under pressure. Expect a mix of experience-based
discussion and a practical case study that digs into troubleshooting and
analytical thinking. **The interview concludes with a live, hands-on
prompt-engineering demonstration**, where you'll share your screen and use a
publicly available AI tool to solve a real-world business problem. This portion is
important—please review the dedicated preparation section below carefully.

**Focus Areas**

- Structured and logical approach to problem-solving
- Ability to break down complex problems into manageable parts
- Troubleshooting methodology and root-cause analysis
- Decision-making with limited information
- Experience integrating or championing AI tools in your work

**How to Prep**

- Prepare examples of times you solved difficult problems, especially ones
  involving ambiguity or incomplete information.
- Reflect on experiences where you adopted, integrated, or championed AI tools or
  workflows—be ready to discuss outcomes and challenges.
- Practice articulating your thought process step-by-step when working through
  unfamiliar scenarios.
- Think about how you prioritize, escalate, and collaborate when facing roadblocks.

**Live Prompt-Engineering Demonstration — Read Carefully**

The final portion of this interview is a **live demo**. You'll share your screen
and use a publicly available AI tool to craft effective prompts and work through a
real-world business problem in real time. You'll be asked to **export your
prompting session as a PDF and send it to your recruiter** afterward, so please
test exporting in advance.

**Technical setup — complete at least 24 hours before your interview:**

- **Open an Incognito / Private browsing window** so your responses aren't
  influenced by prior history. (Chrome/Edge: Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + N; Safari: ⌘ + Shift
  + N; Firefox: Ctrl/⌘ + Shift + P.)
- **Navigate to your preferred allowed AI tool** in that window, and make sure you
  are **not** logged into a personal profile.
- **Test exporting the conversation as a PDF.** Universal method: Ctrl/⌘ + P →
  change destination to "Save as PDF" → Save. (Most tools also have a share/export
  menu.)
- **Close unnecessary apps and programs** before you begin.
- **Confirm you know how to share your screen** on the video-call platform and have
  a stable internet connection.

**Allowed tools**

- **General-purpose chat LLMs:** ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude.ai, Meta.ai.
- **Prototyping / "vibe coding" tools** (if producing a visual prototype): Figma
  Make, Claude Code, v0, Lovable.

**Not allowed**

- Bespoke or custom skills, gems, or prompts built specifically for the interview.
- Tools not available to the general public (e.g., local or custom models).
- Dictation services (e.g., SuperWhisper, Willow Voice, Wispr Flow). If the
  interviewer believes tailored tools/skills are in use, you may be asked to switch
  tools.

**Tips to prepare**

- Treat it as an operational challenge: be structured, use real examples, and show
  critical thinking about AI's opportunities and risks in business contexts.
- Practice iterating on prompts—start broad, then refine to get specific,
  actionable outputs; be ready to adjust when a first answer misses the mark.
- Practice narrating your approach out loud as you prompt, so your reasoning is
  visible.
- Log into your chosen tool and run a full practice session (prompt → refine →
  export to PDF) before interview day.

**Privacy note:** You may hide your AI tool chat history before the interview if
you wish—the interview evaluates your live problem-solving, not prior history. If
you hit any technical issues during setup, contact your recruiter **before**
interview day.

### General Tips *(guide, p. 6 — applies to every round)*

- **Be yourself.** We want to understand how you think and work—there are no trick
  questions.
- **Ask clarifying questions.** It's encouraged and shows thoughtful engagement.
- **Use concrete examples.** Draw from real experiences whenever possible.
- **Prepare questions for your interviewers.** This is also your chance to learn
  about the team and role.

## Part 1 — Experience questions

**Target: 60–90 seconds each.** Lead with the method, use the story as evidence,
stop and let them probe. The follow-up conversation is where you're scored; a
five-minute narrative kills it. Longer versions live in the
[story bank](<./Story Bank - Rounds 4 & 5.md>) for Round 4.

Three habits: say **"I," not "we."** State the number once, flatly, and move on.
**End on the principle, not the outcome.**

The drafts below are for hearing the shape, not memorizing — a recited answer is
audible. **[VERIFY]** = check against what really happened. **[FILL]** = yours to
supply.

### 1. A difficult problem under ambiguity

**Story E — the SOC 2 evidence pipeline.** The cleanest ambiguity story you have,
because the *requirement itself* was undefined.

> "A document pipeline I built last year. Customers going through SOC 2 audits had
> their evidence — policies, approvals, screenshots — only in Chinese, and the
> auditors read English. Someone was translating page by page: slow, and
> inconsistent depending on who did it.
>
> The ambiguity was that the requirement was undefined. 'Auditor-ready' isn't a
> spec, and nobody could tell me which documents would survive OCR — Chinese
> business documents have red seals stamped across the text, handwritten sign-offs,
> tables that don't extract cleanly.
>
> So I made the unknown measurable. I ran real evidence through and sorted the
> output by how badly it degraded, which gave me document *classes* instead of one
> quality number. Clean digital went straight through; stamps, handwriting, and
> complex tables routed to a human rather than being silently degraded.
> **[VERIFY the split.]**
>
> The principle I'd point to: the win isn't accuracy on the average page. It's
> knowing which pages you'll be wrong on *before* you're wrong on them."

That last line is also the right posture toward an AI agent in production. Don't
explain the parallel — let them make it.

*Alternative if they want something older:* Story G, the Marvell bring-up, where
the ambiguity was which layer owned the bug.

### 2. Adopting, integrating, or championing AI tools

Highest-stakes answer in the round — named in the guide here *and* in Round 4, plus
an "AI native" JD bullet. Two readings; if it's ambiguous, ask: *"do you mean AI in
what I've built, or AI in how I work?"*

#### 2a. In the product — the questionnaire assistant

Weight toward **judgment over outcome**. The 60% and 85% are setup; the v1 customers
rejected, and why, is the answer.

> "An AI questionnaire assistant I designed and built at Kaamel. Our enterprise
> customers get security questionnaires from *their* customers — hundreds of
> questions on controls, data handling, incident response — and answering one meant
> a security lead spending days across scattered policy docs.
>
> I designed the retrieval path end to end: ingestion, chunking, embeddings, vector
> index, retrieval, generation. We shipped v1 in mid-2024 and customers didn't trust
> it. They said the quality was low, but when I dug in, that wasn't the real problem
> — **they couldn't see where an answer came from.** These answers go to auditors,
> so an unverifiable claim is legal exposure. Their reaction was rational.
>
> The fix wasn't a better model. I built the traceability layer — every answer links
> back to its source record — made review mandatory rather than a setting, and had
> the system abstain when retrieval came back weak. Effort dropped about 60%, and
> 85% of answers were accepted as-is, measured by the customer's own accept/edit/
> reject click, not by us grading ourselves.
>
> The 15% is the interesting part: two problems in the same costume. Some were
> controls the company had *changed* — a freshness bug. The rest were questions with
> no documented answer anywhere, where the only correct behavior is to say so. One
> accuracy number hides both."

**The line to make sure you say:** *"When someone doesn't trust an AI output, the
fix is usually to make it checkable — not to make it better."*

*On championing:* traceability plus commercial pull — discounted pricing, free
access for partners in exchange for feedback. **[FILL: who internally distrusted it
and what convinced them? That's the missing half of "championed."]**

#### 2b. In your own workflow

Maps almost word-for-word onto a JD preferred qualification (*"prompt/context
engineering, agent orchestration"*). Don't undersell it.

> "ChatGPT, Claude, and Cursor across design, planning, and coding — Claude most
> heavily, to the point where I hit my daily limits.
>
> How I actually use it: on a new initiative I hand it the requirement, have it
> break the product into components, then work through solutions component by
> component. I treat the output as a draft, not an answer — review it, confirm with
> a proof of concept, push back when it's wrong, which is regularly.
>
> The thing that changed my results most: **as a conversation gets long the model
> loses earlier context and quality degrades without telling you.** So I manage
> context deliberately instead of letting a thread sprawl. That's had more effect
> than any prompt wording. Beyond chat I use Skills and MCP servers to extend what
> it can reach."

Almost everyone says "I use Copilot and it saves time." Naming context degradation
as a failure mode you've adapted to is the difference between using a tool and
understanding one. *Caution:* if you cite trying the latest models, make sure the
one you name is actually current.

*Near-certain follow-up — "where should we **not** use AI in a support workflow?"*

> "Anywhere a confident wrong answer is both asymmetric and irreversible. Refunds,
> policy commitments, anything legal or safety-related. My test: if this is wrong,
> can we take it back? If not, the model drafts and a person sends. Same call I made
> on the compliance answers, which is why review there isn't switchable."

### 3. Prioritizing, escalating, collaborating when blocked

They want a **heuristic you actually use**. Lead with the rule, then show it working.

> "I escalate on blast radius and on new information — not on frustration. If impact
> is spreading, or I've learned something the owning team doesn't know yet,
> interrupting them is cheap compared to being wrong. If the only true thing is that
> I'm stuck, that's not an escalation, that's me still working the problem.
>
> For prioritizing: customer impact times reversibility. An irreversible wrong
> action on one customer can outrank a cosmetic issue on a thousand.
>
> Example from Meta. Several teams had each built their own path to the same network
> data and the numbers disagreed. My instinct was to escalate for a ruling, but
> nobody had the information to make that call yet, including me. So I built a
> dashboard comparing every existing path against a unified one, per dataset, per
> metric, live. That turned 'your numbers are wrong' into something people could
> point at together — discrepancies got fixed instead of argued about, and we got
> the unified API to 100% of users. **[VERIFY: dashboard before the migration or
> alongside it?]**
>
> The lesson: the alternative to escalating isn't always doing it yourself.
> Sometimes it's building the thing that makes the decision obvious."

**[FILL — if you have a time you *did* interrupt someone and why, lead with that
instead.** This story is about *avoiding* escalation, so a sharp interviewer may
push for the other case. It also doubles as Round 4's incident story.]

### 4. Working through an unfamiliar scenario

Less a story than a demonstration — they may just hand you
[the case study](#part-2--troubleshooting-case-study) here. Scored on **sequencing,
and why that order**.

> "My sequence, then an example of it.
>
> Scope before depth — how many affected, since when, what changed around then. Then
> the cheapest discriminating check first: not the one nearest my hunch, the one
> that eliminates the most possibilities for the least effort. Separate what
> *changed* from what's always been true, because most incidents are a delta. And
> treat claims in the report as hypotheses — 'nothing was deployed' is something to
> verify, not a fact.
>
> I learned that on silicon bring-up at Marvell. A failure could be in the RTL, the
> board, the firmware, or the test itself, and hardware and software each assumed it
> belonged to the other team. Arguing ownership didn't converge. What worked was
> finding the one observation that split the space. **[FILL: the specific check —
> swapping boards, a known-good part, isolating with a scope.]** Once you can say
> 'this reproduces on two boards, so it isn't the board,' the conversation stops
> being about fault and starts being about the next check.
>
> Same shape as an agent giving a customer a wrong answer: it's our stack or the
> merchant's configuration, and the first move is whichever check tells you which."

**Polish this one first.** It's the only answer that demonstrates the skill rather
than describing it, and it's the natural bridge into Part 2.

### 5. Likely fifth — the hardest thing you've debugged

Not in the guide, but natural in a troubleshooting round. Don't reuse Marvell if
you spent it on Q4.

**Best answer: the AWS us-east-1 outage**, October 2025 — Story H in the
[story bank](<./Story Bank - Rounds 4 & 5.md#h-the-aws-us-east-1-outage--the-incident-story>).
Recent, distributed-systems, and the JD's first responsibility line is
*"independently managing complex outages."* Two beats: **existing EC2 instances kept
running while new launches failed**, so the reflexive restart was the worst move;
and **multi-AZ doesn't protect against a regional control-plane failure.**

If they want something AI-flavoured: a **retrieval bug in the questionnaire
assistant** — nothing errors, no stack trace, the answer is just wrong. **[FILL: a
specific instance.]**

Close either with: **the hardest bugs aren't the ones that crash — they're the ones
that return a plausible answer.**

## Part 2 — Troubleshooting case study

Verbal, no tools. Expect *"A partner says their customers are complaining the agent
stopped responding. What do you do?"*

Same five-step
[RCA framework](<./Interview 3 - Domain Expertise (GenAI).md#the-rca-framework-use-this-live>)
as Round 3, but lead with scoping — in an operational case the first question is
always *how bad is it*.

1. **Scope the blast radius.** One merchant or many? One channel or all? Since when,
   and does it correlate with a deploy or dependency change? Impact right now?
2. **Set severity and communicate.** Does this need a partner status update *before*
   you investigate? Support engineering is judged on comms during an outage as much
   as on the fix.
3. **Diagnose.** Characterize → hypothesize across layers → name the discriminating
   evidence → remediate in three horizons.
4. **Close the loop.** What monitoring would have caught it sooner; what's the
   follow-up.

**Case A — degraded partner integration.** *"Since yesterday, ~1 in 5 of a retail
partner's customers get 'I can't check that right now' instead of order status."*

20% is a suspiciously round partial failure: one bad host in a pool, one region, a
20% canary, or a rate limit hit at peak. Do failures cluster by time of day (rate
limiting) or spread uniformly (bad instance)? Ask if *they* deployed — their API is
the dependency, and "our agent broke" is frequently "their endpoint started
returning 429s." Remediation: immediate = graceful message plus handoff; short-term
= backoff and circuit-breaking; long-term = quota negotiation and a per-partner
error-rate alert.

**Case B — a spike with no obvious cause.** *"Handoff rate jumped 12% → 30%
overnight. Nothing was deployed."*

"Nothing was deployed" is a claim to verify — prompt versions, model versions, index
rebuilds, and config all ship outside the main pipeline. Uniform across merchants
points to a shared component (model, prompt template, index); concentrated points to
a cohort or region. Then: is this a real behavior change or a **measurement**
change? Someone editing the handoff trigger or the metric definition produces an
identical-looking graph. Finally check the handoff *reason* breakdown — "low
confidence" tripling suspects retrieval; "customer requested" tripling means
something upstream is annoying people.

**Raise real-change-vs-measurement-change unprompted in any metrics case.** It's the
mistake people actually make.

### Using your experience here

**This is not an experience question.** Don't answer a case with a story — you spent
those in Part 1. But one *clause* mid-sentence upgrades a generic move:

| Move | The clause that upgrades it |
|---|---|
| "Check whether their endpoint started erroring" | "…I've had 'our thing is broken' turn out to be the partner's endpoint returning 429s" |
| "I'd want per-partner error rates, not global" | "…global metrics hide one tenant being fully down — I built the discrepancy dashboards at Meta for that blind spot" |
| "Real change or measurement change?" | "…I've watched a metric 'move' because someone changed the definition upstream" |
| "I'd tell the partner before they tell us" | "…better they hear our pipeline's limits from me than from their auditor" |

**The case is the subject; the experience is an adjective.** One clause, never a
paragraph.

## Part 3 — Live prompt-engineering demo

### Pre-flight, 24h before

Do all of this in one sitting, **before** any content prep.

- [ ] **Incognito window** (`⌘⇧N`, Firefox `⌘⇧P`), **not** logged into a personal
      profile.
- [ ] **Full practice run**: prompt → refine → refine → export.
- [ ] **Test PDF export** (`⌘P` → "Save as PDF"). Verify it contains the *whole*
      conversation — long chats clip. Know where it saves.
- [ ] **Screen sharing** works; know single-window vs. whole-screen.
- [ ] **Close everything else.** You're sharing your screen and the recruiter gets
      the transcript.
- [ ] Internet stable; phone hotspot as backup.

Anything fails → contact the recruiter **before** interview day. The guide says so,
and flagging early is neutral-to-positive.

**Allowed:** ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude.ai, Meta.ai; plus Figma Make, Claude Code, v0,
Lovable *if you need a visual prototype*. **Not allowed:** custom skills/gems/prompts
built for the interview, non-public models, dictation tools.

**Pick a general-purpose chat LLM** unless the prompt demands a visual artifact —
the round is about prompting, the transcript is the deliverable, and PDF export from
a chat UI is trivial. **Meta.ai** is a reasonable choice (Meta's own product, Llama,
which the JD names) *only if you've rehearsed on it*. Fumbling an unfamiliar UI on a
shared screen costs more than the goodwill. Rehearse all four scenarios on whichever
you pick.

### What's actually scored

The focus areas are structured problem-solving, decomposition, troubleshooting
method, and decisions under limited information. **Not** prompt-engineering jargon.
The guide's framing: **"treat it as an operational challenge."**

- **The first prompt shouldn't be perfect.** Start broad, refine. One prompt → one
  great answer teaches them nothing. **Plan on three-plus iterations.**
- **Narrate.** They see the screen, not your head. Silence while typing is the most
  common way to lose this round.
- **Critique the output.** *"This is 80% there, but it invented a metric we don't
  have and it's too long for a partner email"* is the highest-signal thing you can
  say — it shows you don't take model output at face value.
- **Land on a usable artifact.** A runbook, a triage rubric, a system prompt. Not a
  wall of text.
- **Clock:** 4–6 exchanges in ~15 min.

### Narration: four beats per iteration

**Intent** ("starting broad so I don't anchor it to my assumptions") → **Prompt**
(read key parts aloud) → **Critique** (name the specific problems) → **Refine**
(state which constraint you're adding and why).

Lines worth having ready:

- "I'm giving it a role and an audience first — those change output more than
  anything else."
- "I deliberately didn't give it a format yet. I want to see what it thinks is
  natural before I constrain it."
- "That's a hallucination — it cited a metric we never defined."
- "This is good enough to iterate on rather than restart."
- "In a real workflow I'd validate this against the actual data before it goes to a
  partner — the model's confidence isn't evidence."

That last one matters: knowing where AI output needs human verification is a hiring
signal for a support role, not a hedge.

### The scaffold

Same six blocks as the
[system-prompt anatomy](<./Interview 3 - Domain Expertise (GenAI).md#system-prompt-anatomy>)
in Round 3. One structure across both rounds reads as method, not improvisation.

```text
ROLE:         Who the model is, and whose interests it serves
CONTEXT:      The facts it may use — and that it may use nothing else
INSTRUCTIONS: The task, decomposed, in priority order
EXAMPLES:     One or two, if format matters
OUTPUT:       Format, length, audience reading level
GUARDRAILS:   What to do when information is missing; what never to assert
```

Don't type all six up front. Start with ROLE + INSTRUCTIONS and add blocks as the
output reveals gaps — narrating *which block and why* is the performance:

> "It invented three statistics. That means my CONTEXT was too thin and there was no
> GUARDRAIL about unknowns. I'll add both."

### Two definitions to have ready

**A prompt** = *the entire input sequence the model sees on one call.* The model is
stateless — no memory, no clock, no database. In production that sequence is
assembled from the system prompt, prior turns, retrieved chunks, tool definitions,
tool results, and the user message; internally there's no boundary between them.

Why this framing earns something: it explains every agent failure in one move. Wrong
about "is the sale still on?" because nothing states today's date. Quotes a stale
policy because the index holds the old chunk. Drops a constraint from twelve turns
ago because it's buried. Hallucinates because there's a gap and the weights fill it.
**A model doesn't know what it wasn't told.**

**Prompt engineering** = designing that input so output is *reliable*, not good once.
Anyone gets a good answer on the third try; the job is an acceptable answer on the
first, across millions of merchants including the strange ones. Five activities:
**specification** (role, task, audience, output contract), **context engineering**
(what goes in the window and what stays out — now the harder half), **guardrails**
(*"if the context doesn't contain the answer, say so and offer a human"* is the
highest-value sentence in most production prompts), **demonstration**, and
**evaluation** — the part people skip, and what separates a practitioner from an
enthusiast.

*What it mostly isn't anymore:* magic phrasing. "World-class expert," "take a deep
breath," offering a tip — real on 2023-era models, dead weight now. Saying it will
date you.

*Follow-up worth having — prompt vs. RAG vs. fine-tuning.* Prompting changes
behavior, RAG supplies knowledge, fine-tuning changes defaults. Reach in that order.
You have standing here: you built a full RAG path and can say why retrieval beat
fine-tuning for a policy corpus that changes.

### Where your experience lands mid-demo

One sentence, then back to the screen. You already told the stories in Part 1.

| When you're… | Say |
|---|---|
| Adding GUARDRAILS | "The block I care most about. On the compliance assistant, abstention when retrieval was weak mattered more than answer quality — a blank is recoverable, a confident wrong answer to an auditor isn't." |
| Asked how you'd know it works | "Accept/edit/reject rate, not a self-assessment. Ours was the customer's own click in the review queue — 85% accepted." |
| The model invents a fact | "What actually fixed this for us wasn't a better prompt — it was linking every answer to its source record so a reviewer could check it in two seconds." |
| Asked about limits | "Two different things get called 'wrong': controls that had *changed* — a freshness problem — and questions with no documented answer, which is abstention. Different fixes." |

**Keep the numbers honest.** 60% and 85% are what you have, they're good, and
inflating invites a follow-up you can't survive.

### Rehearsal 1 — Partner-facing troubleshooting guide

*"A partner integrating the Business Agent reports it gives wrong order statuses.
Produce something that helps them."* Most on-the-nose scenario. Rehearse first.

**1 — broad, to see its framing.**

```text
I support businesses integrating an AI customer-service agent into their
messaging channels. A partner reports the agent sometimes gives customers
incorrect order status. Before we write anything: what are the most likely
causes, organized by system layer?
```

*"I want a cause list first, so the guide is structured around real failure modes
rather than my guesses."*

**2 — constrain to what we know.**

```text
Good list, but narrow it. Constraints:
- The partner's own order API is the source of truth; we call it per request.
- We do not cache order data.
- The agent has retrieval over their FAQ/policy docs, not over order data.
Re-rank given those constraints and drop what's now impossible. For each
remaining cause, give the one log field or check that would confirm it.
```

*"The important move — eliminating whole branches with facts. And a cause list
without a way to test it isn't actionable."*

**3 — the artifact.**

```text
Now write this as a self-serve troubleshooting guide for the partner's engineer.

AUDIENCE: a backend engineer at a mid-size retailer, not an AI specialist.
FORMAT: a numbered decision tree. Each step is one check, its expected result,
and where to go next. Max one page.
GUARDRAIL: only reference checks they can run on their own systems — they have
no access to our internal logs. If a step needs our logs, say "contact support
with your conversation ID" instead.
```

*"A guide that tells a partner to check logs they can't see is worse than no guide."*

**4 — pressure-test.**

```text
Review your own guide as if you were that partner engineer. Where would you get
stuck, and what's ambiguous? Then give me the revised version.
```

**Close:** read the artifact aloud, then say what you'd verify before sending —
that the log field names are real, and a partner-facing teammate reads it for tone.

### Rehearsal 2 — Triage a ticket backlog

*"Here are 40 partner tickets from this week. What's going on, what do we
prioritize?"* Tests analytical structure more than wordcraft.

**1 — taxonomy, not summary.**

```text
I'm going to paste support tickets from businesses using our AI customer-service
agent. First: propose a categorization scheme that would let me spot systemic
issues, before you look at the content. What dimensions should I classify on?
```

*"Axes before analysis. If it summarizes first I get a narrative, and narratives
hide distribution."* Push it to include **"product defect vs. policy/expectation
issue"** — the axis that stops you over-counting bugs.

**2 — classify, with an unknown bucket.**

```text
[paste tickets]

Classify each ticket on those dimensions. Output a table, one row per ticket.

GUARDRAILS:
- If a ticket lacks enough information for a dimension, put "UNKNOWN" — do not
  infer.
- Do not merge tickets that merely sound similar; only group them if they
  describe the same observable symptom.
```

*"Left alone it will confidently classify everything, and I get clean-looking data
that's partly fabricated."*

**3 — counts to recommendation.**

```text
Aggregate: highest volume, highest severity. Recommend a priority order for
engineering. Weight by merchants affected (not ticket count — one loud merchant
filing ten tickets is one merchant), whether a workaround exists, and whether
the trend is growing. State confidence and what data would raise it.
```

*"Tickets-per-merchant is the trap — the loudest partner isn't the biggest problem."*

**Close:** pull real error rates for the top two categories. If tickets and telemetry
disagree, that gap is itself the finding.

### Rehearsal 3 — Write the agent's system prompt

*"A small business is onboarding. Draft the agent's instructions."* Highest overlap
with Round 3 — build it live rather than reciting the template.

**1 — deliberately thin, to expose gaps.**

```text
Write a system prompt for an AI assistant that handles customer messages for a
small florist on WhatsApp.
```

**2 — critique aloud, then fix.** It will almost certainly miss: the current date,
tool-failure behavior, the abstention rule, channel formatting, and the handoff
trigger. **Naming those gaps out loud is the interview.**

```text
Good skeleton but it's missing the failure paths. Revise using:
ROLE / CONTEXT / INSTRUCTIONS / EXAMPLES / OUTPUT FORMAT / GUARDRAILS.

- Inject today's date as a variable; the model has no clock and will otherwise
  get "is the sale still on" wrong.
- Answer only from provided context. If it lacks the answer, say so and offer a
  human — never fill the gap.
- Any claim about a specific order, price, or stock level requires a tool call.
  If the tool fails, say so; never guess the result.
- Plain text only, no markdown — this renders in WhatsApp.
- Label few-shot examples as examples so they aren't mistaken for facts.
```

Narrate the date and markdown points specifically — small, concrete, unmistakably
from someone who's shipped this.

**3 — red-team.**

```text
Act as an adversarial customer. Give me five messages that would make this prompt
produce a wrong, unsafe, or off-brand response. For each, say which instruction
failed to prevent it.
```

*"Writing the prompt is easy; finding where it breaks is the work."* Patch the top
two holes and stop.

### Rehearsal 4 — A business problem with no code in it

*"Merchants in a new market are churning in their first month. Figure out what to do
about it."* **Rehearse this even though it feels off-theme** — the guide says
"real-world **business** problem," and if it turns out to be go-to-market or comms,
having only technical rehearsals means improvising the format you're scored on.
15 minutes to prepare.

**1 — refuse to answer yet.**

```text
I support businesses using an AI customer-service agent. Merchants in a new
market are churning in their first month. Before proposing anything: what are the
plausible causes, and what data would distinguish between them?
```

*"Same move as a technical case. A list of fixes for an undiagnosed problem is
guesswork with formatting."*

**2 — separate knowns from assumptions.**

```text
Split that into: (a) verifiable with data we plausibly already have, (b) needs new
instrumentation, (c) only a merchant conversation would answer. Mark any claim
you're inferring rather than deriving.
```

*"Business analysis is where models are most fluent and least grounded, so I want
the inference boundary drawn explicitly."*

**3 — artifact with an audience.**

```text
Write this as a one-page brief for a product lead who has 3 minutes. Lead with the
recommendation, then reasoning, then what would change my mind. State confidence
on each claim. No filler.
```

*"'What would change my mind' is what makes a recommendation reviewable instead of a
pitch."*

**Close:** is the churn real or a measurement artifact — same distinction as
[Case B](#part-2--troubleshooting-case-study).

### When the model gives you a bad answer

The guide says be ready to adjust; the interviewer may be *hoping* for this, because
recovery is more informative than success.

| Symptom | Cause | Fix, named out loud |
|---|---|---|
| Invented facts or fields | Thin CONTEXT, no unknown guardrail | Add context + "if you don't know, say UNKNOWN" |
| Generic, could-be-anyone | No ROLE or audience | Add role + audience + one concrete detail |
| Right content, wrong shape | No OUTPUT block | Specify format, length, structure |
| Too long | No length constraint, open question | Constrain length *and* narrow the ask |
| Ignored an instruction | Too many in one prompt | Split, or number and prioritize |
| Confidently wrong reasoning | Asked for a conclusion without steps | Reasoning first, then conclude |
| Drifted from constraints | Long thread, constraints fell out of attention | Restate them; don't assume it remembers |

- **Don't restart when 70% is good.** "I'll keep this skeleton and fix two things" is
  what an experienced practitioner does; restarting reads as not knowing how to steer.
- **Do restart when the framing is wrong.** Patching a misunderstood goal makes it
  worse. Deciding *which* situation you're in, and saying why, is the skill.
