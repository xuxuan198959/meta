### Interview 4: Hiring Manager

**What to Expect**

This 45-minute conversation with the hiring manager focuses on your motivations,
career journey, and how you approach supporting stakeholders and customers. It's an
opportunity to share how you've grown, handled challenges, and delivered impact in
previous roles.

**Focus Areas**

- Customer and stakeholder orientation
- Service mindset and commitment to quality support
- Career growth, adaptability, and self-direction
- Motivation for this role and alignment with the team's mission
- Examples of business impact and overcoming challenges

**How to Prep**

- Reflect on your career transitions—be ready to explain the "why" behind key
  decisions you've made.
- Prepare examples that demonstrate how you've prioritized stakeholder needs while
  balancing organizational goals.
- Think about constructive feedback you've received and how you've applied it.
- Be ready to share what excites you about this role and working on AI-driven
  business solutions.

### General Tips *(guide, p. 6 — applies to every round)*

- **Be yourself.** We want to understand how you think and work—there are no trick
  questions.
- **Ask clarifying questions.** It's encouraged and shows thoughtful engagement.
- **Use concrete examples.** Draw from real experiences whenever possible.
- **Prepare questions for your interviewers.** This is also your chance to learn
  about the team and role.

## First: the story matrix

**Seven stories, reused across ~30 possible questions.** Nobody prepares thirty
answers; you prepare a handful and re-aim them. The
[filled-in matrix](<./Story Bank - Rounds 4 & 5.md#the-story-matrix>) is in the
story bank — work from that, not from a blank grid.

The seven, by handle: **A** questionnaire assistant · **B** unified data API ·
**C** cell-performance prediction · **F** compliance platform · **H** AWS outage ·
**I** the 2023 product-selection call · **J** learning by conversation.

> **D and E were deleted, and both gaps are now covered without new stories.**
> D covered *technical → non-technical*; that question is now answered by a new angle
> on **A** — the lawyer who called the output "suspicious," the three-way split of what
> was actually wrong, and the source link as the explanation. E covered *customer over
> an internal goal*; that's now **B**, told as a sequencing decision — the unified API
> was already on the roadmap, the users wouldn't wait for it, so the quick fix shipped

>
> **The cost is concentration.** A carries six questions in this round and B carries
> four. Each telling is a genuinely different angle, but the don't-tell-it-twice rule
> matters more than it would with nine stories — see the reuse note at the end of this
> section.

**All seven are Kaamel or Meta**, and the early hardware roles don't appear anywhere
— not as a story, and not as chronology in the career walkthrough. They're the
oldest thing on the résumé and the furthest from this job; the walkthrough starts at
Meta in 2018.

Rules the set has to satisfy, and where it currently stands:

- **At least two with numbers** — A (60% / 85%), F ($7k/customer/yr), B (100%). ✓
- **At least one that went badly** — A's launch that wouldn't sell, and H being
  found by a customer rather than by monitoring. ✓
- **At least one recent** — A, F, H. ✓
- **At least three involving people outside your team** — B, C, H. ✓ *(exactly three
  since D was deleted — no margin left; the next cut breaks this rule)*
- **One AI story** — A, with J as the workflow half. ✓
- **Feedback you received** — ✓, and it isn't one of the seven. Your manager at Meta
  told you that you didn't give constructive feedback and that it was a leadership
  weakness; the payoff beat is the PM conversation on the data-layer project.
- **Technical → non-technical / adapting to audiences** — ✓ via A, the lawyer
  conversation.
- **Customer over internal goal** — ✓ via B, told as sequencing.

For each story, four things on paper and no more:

```text
Situation:  one sentence of setup, and why it was hard
Action:     what YOU specifically did (not "we")
Outcome:    the result, with a number if one exists
Learning:   what you'd do differently, or what it changed about how you work
```

The guide asks for "situation, your actions, outcome" — STAR without the separate
Task step. Keep the situation to one or two sentences; the most common failure in
these rounds is three minutes of context before any action.

**On reusing Round 2 material:** different interviewer, so reuse is fine and expected.
What can't repeat is the same story told the same way twice inside *this* round.

**The angles, so you can keep them apart under pressure.** A is drafted six ways:
requirements (proudest, and decision without enough information), the launch that
wouldn't sell (frustrated customer, major setback, time you failed), the lawyer
conversation (technical → non-technical), ownership (end to end), scope-by-exposure
(largest scope), and the abstention rule (where *not* to use AI). B is drafted four
ways: discovery (problem nobody asked you to fix), sequencing (customer over internal
goal), the choice not to escalate (escalate vs. keep working), and the PM conversation
(difficult feedback).

**Two clusters are close enough to notice.** *Frustrated customer* / *major setback* /
*time you failed* are all the mid-2024 launch — if two come up, say so out loud and
lead with the different beat. *Problem nobody asked you to fix* and *escalate vs. keep
working* both run through the discrepancy dashboard; the first should stay on discovery
and the meeting, the second on the reasoning about escalating.

**C and F are unused in the drafted bank**, which makes them the spares. If a repeat is
about to happen, C (cell-performance prediction with research scientists → carriers) can
take a cross-functional or technical-to-non-technical question, and F (the compliance
platform, $7k per customer per year, replaced third-party tools) can take impact or
end-to-end ownership.

## The AI story (highest priority)

Named in **both** the Round 2 focus areas and this round's prep bullets, plus the
JD line *"leverage AI tools to accelerate troubleshooting … with an 'AI native'
mindset."* Two mentions and a JD bullet means it isn't a throwaway.

**Two readings, and they want different stories.** If the question is ambiguous,
ask: *"do you mean AI in what I've built, or AI in how I work?"*

- **AI in what you've built → [Story A](<./Story Bank - Rounds 4 & 5.md#a-questionnaire-assistant--the-ai-story>).**
  For this round lead with the *adoption* arc, because "championed" is the guide's
  word: launched, customers wouldn't buy it, we discounted, **the discount did
  nothing**, the real objection was verifiability rather than quality, and the fix
  was surfacing the source on every answer rather than improving the model.
- **AI in how you work → [Story J](<./Story Bank - Rounds 4 & 5.md#j-learning-by-conversation--learned-something-fast>),**
  plus the current-workflow probe underneath it — ChatGPT, Claude and Cursor across
  design, planning and coding, with **context degradation over a long thread** named
  as the failure mode you've adapted to. That specific observation is what separates
  using a tool from understanding one.

**What a weak version sounds like:** "I use Copilot and ChatGPT a lot, they save me
time." True of everyone; distinguishes nothing.

**What the strong version has, and where yours has it:**

**A is a product, not a workflow** — a shipped, sold, externally-adopted product,
which is the stronger of the two things "championed an AI tool" can mean. Don't
shrink it into "here's how I use AI at my desk"; that's Story J's job. The tell that
you've framed it right: the story has *customers* in it, and a launch that failed.

| Requirement | Where A supplies it |
|---|---|
| A specific product, not a tool name | An AI questionnaire assistant sold to enterprise customers — RAG over their knowledge base, answers traceable to source, human review in the loop |
| Adoption beyond yourself | Paying customers, via traceability and a feedback-for-access trade — Drata ended up reciprocal |
| An honest limitation | The 15%: stale controls (freshness) vs. no documented answer (abstention) |
| How you handled trust | The whole story. Provenance you can't see isn't provenance |
| An outcome | 60% less prep effort, 85% accepted on the customer's own click |

**Likely follow-ups:**

- *"Where should we* not *use AI in a support workflow?"* → Anywhere a confident
  wrong answer is both asymmetric and irreversible: refunds, policy commitments,
  anything legal or safety-related. The test: if this is wrong, can we take it
  back? If not, the model drafts and a person sends. Same call as the abstention
  rule in [Interview 3](<./Interview 3 - Domain Expertise (GenAI).md>).
- *"How did you measure whether it helped?"* → The accept/edit/reject click in the
  review queue. Their judgment, not ours.
- *"How do you keep people from over-trusting it?"* → Make the uncertainty visible
  in the output — cite sources, abstain when retrieval is weak, keep a human in the
  loop where it's irreversible.
- *"Models got better anyway — how do you know it was your changes?"* → **Expect
  this**, and the answer is in the story bank. Don't claim the model didn't help.

**The gap to name honestly:** internal adoption. Customer adoption is well
covered; who *on the team* you brought along isn't, and "championed" points at
both. See [What's still open](<./Story Bank - Rounds 4 & 5.md#whats-still-open>).

## Focus areas, and what's underneath them

Underneath all five of the guide's focus areas is one question: **do you actually
want a support engineering role, or is this a way into Meta?** Support orgs get a lot of the latter and the
HM will be probing for it. Every answer should be consistent with someone who
finds partner-facing operational work genuinely interesting.

**One caution specific to your bank.** Story I is a strategy story — market
analysis, moats, product selection — in an operational loop. It's your best
evidence of judgment on thin information, and it's a liability if it sounds like
you'd rather be founding a company. The insurance is the line that frames the
method as engineering: *"same procedure I'd run on a hard bug — I couldn't get the
measurement I wanted, so I took the one I could get and used it to rule things
out."* Don't tell that story without it.

### Motivation and journey

#### ★ "Walk me through your career. Why the transitions?"

> I'll start where the relevant part starts — Meta, 2018.
>
> I spent about three and a half years on a carrier-facing partner portal. Two things
> there shaped what I want to do now. First, several teams were each fetching the same
> data their own way, and their numbers disagreed. I led the work to consolidate that
> into one API, and I built the dashboards that showed where the datasets diverged. We
> got it to 100% of users. Second, I worked with research scientists to ship a feature
> that flagged underperforming network cells and predicted the gain from upgrading
> them — carriers used it to decide where to spend real money.
>
> Neither of those was a new feature. Both were messy, shared problems. That's the work
> I kept choosing.
>
> Then I took about a year and a half out for a family matter. That's the whole of it.
>
> When I was ready to come back, AI had just changed what was possible to build. I
> wanted to be building with it, so I joined Kaamel in late 2023. I built an AI
> questionnaire assistant — RAG over the customer's own documents, drafting their
> security and compliance answers. It cut prep effort by 60%, and 85% of its answers
> are accepted after the customer reviews them.
>
> It's a small company, so I got something I couldn't get at Meta at my level then. I
> design the system, I'm in the room with the customer, and I own it in production.
>
> That's why I'm here. I've spent two years on one problem — an LLM being confidently
> wrong in front of somebody's customer — and I want to work on it at a scale I can't
> get anywhere else.

#### ★ "Why this role? Why support engineering specifically?"

> Two reasons.
>
> The first is that I want to keep working on AI, and I want to do it at scale. That's
> what this role has that I can't get where I am.
>
> The second is the work itself. At Kaamel I'm not only building — I'm in the room with
> the customer, and I'm the one on the incident. I've learned that I like that seat.
> It's the one place that's connected to everybody: the PM, the customer, and the
> engineers.
>
> And troubleshooting an AI product is the most interesting version of that job. With
> normal software, a bug is a stack trace — you read the code and you find it. With an
> AI product, the question is usually "was this answer good enough, and for who?" You
> can't answer that from the code. You have to know the architecture well enough to
> have a real hypothesis — was it retrieval, was it the prompt, was it a tool call, was
> the data just stale — and then you have to go talk to the person who got the answer
> to find out which one it was.
>
> So I don't think of this as a normal support role. It takes the engineering depth and
> it takes the communication, and that combination is what I want to get better at.

#### ★ "Why Meta, and why now?"

> Three reasons.
>
> First, the scale is specific, not a slogan. Business Agent went global a few months
> ago and business-AI conversations are running around ten million a week — roughly ten
> times what they were a quarter before. At Kaamel, when the model gets something
> wrong, one customer's auditor sees it. At that volume, the rare failure isn't rare
> anymore. It happens thousands of times a day. That's the version of the problem I
> want to work on.
>
> Second, I've worked here before, so I'm not guessing about it. What I remember most
> is how easy it was to get things done — you find the person who owns something and
> you get a decision that week. I've worked in places where that isn't true, and it's a
> bigger difference than people expect. I left for a family matter, not because I
> wanted to leave.
>
> Third, the timing. When I came back to work in 2023, AI had just changed what was
> possible, and I've spent the two years since building AI products in production. The
> support layer for something like Business Agent is being built right now. I'd rather
> help build it than maintain it.

#### *Follow-up:* "It sounds like you want to be a PM."

> No. I want to be the engineer who talks to the customer. The PM decides what we
> build. I want to figure out why it's wrong — that's a debugging job before it's a
> product job.

#### *Follow-up:* "You left Meta once. What's different now?"

> I left for family, not because I was unhappy. What's different is that I now have two
> years of AI in production that I didn't have then, and this is a role that uses it.

#### "What are you looking for in your next role that you don't have today?"

> Scale is part of it — I've said that already, so I won't relitigate it. The part I
> haven't said is people.
>
> At Kaamel we built an AI product from scratch and worked out the approach together,
> and I'm proud of how it turned out. But it's one approach, figured out by a small
> team. I don't know how it compares to how this is being done elsewhere.
>
> So what I want is to see how Meta does it — engineers solving the same problems at a
> much bigger size, and probably differently. I expect that changes how I think about
> building these products, and I'd rather learn that than keep refining the one
> approach I know.
>
> The other piece is the environment. What I remember about Meta is people being
> motivated, acting immediately, and really
> owning what they own. That's where I do my best work.

#### "Where do you want to be in a few years?"

> Concretely: I want to be the person people come to when an AI product isn't
> performing well enough and nobody can say why yet.
>
> That's two skills, and they're different. One is depth in AI product architecture —
> knowing the system well enough that I can look at a bad output and have a short list
> of where it could have gone wrong. The other is diagnosis: getting from a vague
> complaint to the component that's actually failing.
>
> The third piece is what makes those useful. I want to be someone who can lead and
> communicate across backgrounds — customers, PMs, engineers, infra — because building
> and supporting an AI product takes all of them, and somebody has to hold that group
> together.
>
> If I'm doing that at Meta's scale in a few years, that's the job I want.

#### "What would make you leave a job?"

> Honestly, the main one is the work drifting away from AI. That's what I've pointed my
> career at for the last two years, and it's what I want to keep going deeper on. If I
> ended up on something with no AI in it, I'd want to move — inside the company first,
> but I'd want to move.
>
> Short of that, I don't leave easily. I was at Meta three and a half years, I've been
> at Kaamel two, and I left Meta for a family matter, not for another job.

### Customer / stakeholder orientation

#### "Tell me about a time you prioritized a customer's needs against a competing internal goal. How did you decide?"

> My default is the customer. I think a customer-centric org wins, so when the two pull
> against each other I start from their side. But customer-first isn't order-taking. I
> ask whether the ask benefits most of our customers or a small subset — if it's broad,
> there usually isn't a real conflict.
>
> The example is the data layer at Meta. Several teams were each pulling the same data
> through their own API, and the numbers disagreed. People were making decisions on
> figures that didn't match.
>
> We already had the answer on the roadmap — one unified API behind all of it, which
> would fix the discrepancies properly. But it was months out, and the users didn't
> want to wait months. They wanted their numbers to agree now.
>
> So I prioritized the quick fix and improved the existing APIs to close the gaps. It
> worked, and I knew it wasn't the long-term answer: every one of those APIs still had
> to be maintained on its own, so the cost of ownership stayed high.
>
> Then I built the unified API as planned, with dashboards that surface any divergence
> between datasets. That rolled out to 100% of users.
>
> The decision was about order, not either/or. Choosing the customer meant taking on
> work I knew I'd throw away, so they weren't blocked while we did the right thing. It
> didn't mean skipping the right thing.

#### "Describe a time you dealt with a very frustrated customer or partner."

> The launch of the questionnaire assistant, mid-2024. The customers weren't angry at
> me personally — they were frustrated with the product, and they showed it by not
> buying it.
>
> What they told us was that answer quality was low. We had a price lever, so we used
> it. We discounted. It did nothing — not a small effect, none. That's what told me
> we'd misread the objection.
>
> So I went back and asked them properly. These answers go to their customers'
> auditors. A plausible answer they couldn't verify wasn't an inconvenience, it was
> legal exposure. They weren't declining to pay for a mediocre tool. They were
> declining to put their name on something they couldn't check.
>
> The fix was a product change, not a model change. The system could already trace
> every answer back to the source record — the customer just couldn't see it.
> Provenance you can't see isn't provenance. So we surfaced the source on every answer,
> made human review mandatory instead of a setting, and traded early access to a few
> customers for a real feedback loop.
>
> 85% of answers now get accepted as-is, measured by the customer's own
> accept-edit-reject click. And the discount did start working — but only once it was
> buying feedback instead of adoption.

#### "How do you decide when to escalate versus keep working a problem yourself?"

> My rule is that I escalate when I have information the decision-maker doesn't. Not
> when I'm stuck.
>
> Being stuck is my problem to work. The reason to go up is that a decision has to be
> made above my level and I'm holding the thing that makes it decidable. Escalating
> without that just moves an argument up a level.
>
> The clearest case where I didn't escalate was the data layer at Meta. Several teams
> disagreed about whose numbers were right, and my instinct was to take it up for a
> ruling. I didn't, because nobody had the information to make that call — including
> me. So I built the discrepancy dashboard first: every path against the unified one,
> per metric, live. That turned it from an argument about teams into a question about
> data, and it largely answered itself.
>
> The flip side matters more operationally. In an incident, the moment I can see the
> blast radius is bigger than my scope, I escalate immediately. That's information
> other people need, and waiting around to look competent is expensive.

#### "Tell me about a time you said no to a stakeholder."

> Late 2023, a few weeks into Kaamel. The team was already building an AI resume
> generator, and I thought we should stop.
>
> My reason was first-hand. I'd been job hunting not long before and used ChatGPT to
> write my resume. I was the target customer, and I wouldn't have paid for it.
>
> But that's one person's experience, and as a new hire an opinion is the weakest thing
> you can bring into a room. So I did the competitor study and the market sizing before
> I said anything. When I raised it, I said plainly which part was evidence and which
> part was me, and I put the question on the product rather than on the people who'd
> been building it.
>
> The project was stopped. What made that work wasn't that I was persuasive — it's that
> I wasn't asking anyone to trust my judgment. I put the same information in front of
> everyone.

#### "How do you explain a technical problem to a non-technical partner?"

> Customers weren't buying the questionnaire assistant, so I went to talk to a lawyer
> at one of them — she'd be the person on the hook if an answer was wrong.
>
> Her feedback was that the answer quality was suspicious, and maybe AI just wasn't
> smart enough for this work. I can't act on that, so I asked for concrete examples.
> She pulled up specific questions and answers, and what she said was that she couldn't
> understand where they came from.
>
> When I went through them, they were three different problems. Some were questions we
> had nothing in the knowledge base for. Some had information that was out of date. And
> some were correct given what was in the knowledge base — just not intuitive to
> someone reading them.
>
> She didn't have the vocabulary for that. Context, knowledge base, model — none of
> those meant anything to her. So I didn't try to explain the architecture. I gave her
> one distinction: is the model wrong, or do we simply not have the answer written down
> anywhere? Those look identical in the output and they're opposite problems. One of
> them is our system. The other one is her company's documentation.
>
> Then I stopped explaining and showed her. We surfaced the source link on every
> answer. Once she could click through to the passage an answer came from, she could
> tell which case she was looking at without me — and for the correct-but-unintuitive
> ones, the link did the entire job.
>
> That's what I took from it. With a non-technical partner the win usually isn't a
> better explanation. It's finding the one distinction they actually need, and then
> making the evidence visible so they don't need me in the room.

### Impact and challenge

#### "What's the project you're proudest of, and what was your specific contribution?"

> The questionnaire assistant at Kaamel. Enterprise customers answer the same security
> questions by hand, pulling from policy documents scattered across the company, and
> those answers go to their customers' auditors. So it isn't a productivity tool with a
> quality problem. It's a document with legal exposure attached.
>
> What I was handed was "the answers have to be high quality and satisfy the customer."
> That's an adjective, not a spec, and the customers couldn't define it either. So my
> first contribution was turning it into one. I went to customers and to the lawyers
> who'd be on the hook, and I didn't ask them what quality meant — I asked what they
> cared about most. I got the same answer every time: is it correct, and if this gets
> challenged, how do I defend it?
>
> Nobody said the word traceability. Translating "defend" into something I could build
> and test was my job. It came out as four properties: every answer grounded in the
> knowledge base rather than the model's general knowledge; every answer traceable to
> the record it came from; when the knowledge base has nothing, no assumption — surface
> it as unknown and hand it to a person; and human review in the loop, so every miss
> comes back as signal.
>
> Then I designed and built it — ingestion through to the review queue.
>
> 60% less preparation effort, and 85% of answers accepted as-is, measured by the
> customer's own accept-edit-reject click rather than by our opinion of ourselves.
>
> The part I'm proudest of is the requirements, not the architecture. It worked because
> we got right what "good" meant to someone who couldn't put it into words.

#### "Tell me about a time you owned something end to end."

> The questionnaire assistant. I owned it from the requirements to what happens after a
> customer clicks reject.
>
> I gathered the requirements myself — customers and the lawyers who'd be liable for a
> wrong answer, because "high quality" wasn't a spec and nobody could define it for me.
> I designed the architecture: ingestion, chunking, embeddings, vector indexing,
> semantic retrieval, answer generation, and the human review queue. I built it. I ran
> it in production in front of paying customers.
>
> And I owned the parts people don't count as engineering. When it didn't sell, I was
> the one going back to customers to find out why. When 15% of answers were getting
> rejected, I was the one going through them to work out what they had in common.
>
> That's what end-to-end means to me — not that I wrote every layer, but that there was
> nobody else the problem belonged to at any stage. 60% less prep effort, 85% accepted
> on the customer's own click.

#### "Describe a major challenge or setback and how you handled it."

> We launched the questionnaire assistant in mid-2024 and customers wouldn't buy it.
> That's the setback — not a bad quarter, a product that didn't sell.
>
> The stated complaint was that answer quality was low. Models were weaker then, so it
> was believable. We had a price lever and we pulled it. The discount did nothing. Not
> a small effect — none.
>
> That's what told me we'd misdiagnosed it. A price change that moves nothing means
> price was never the objection. So I went back to customers and asked what they were
> actually worried about. These answers go to auditors. A plausible answer they
> couldn't verify was legal exposure. They weren't declining to pay for a mediocre
> tool. They were declining to put their name on something they couldn't check.
>
> The fix was a product change, not a model change. The system could already trace
> every answer to its source — the customer just couldn't see it. We surfaced the
> source on every answer, made human review mandatory instead of optional, and traded
> early access for a real feedback loop with a few customers.
>
> It recovered. 85% acceptance, measured by the customer's own click. And the discount
> started working once it was buying feedback rather than adoption.

#### "Tell me about a time you failed. What changed afterward?"

> The discount. When the questionnaire assistant didn't sell, customers told us the
> answer quality was low, and we responded by cutting the price. It did nothing at all.
>
> The failure wasn't the discount itself, it was the reasoning. We took a stated
> complaint at face value and reached for the lever we happened to have, instead of
> testing whether we understood the objection. Months of a launch went into that.
>
> What it turned out to be was verifiability. These answers go to auditors, and a
> plausible answer nobody can check is a liability, not a bargain. Cheaper doesn't help
> with that. Neither, mostly, does better.
>
> What changed is that I don't act on a stated complaint until I have something that
> confirms it. When 15% of answers were still being rejected later on, I didn't
> theorize — I went through them one at a time and sorted them. They split into two
> classes that need opposite fixes: controls that had changed since we indexed them,
> and questions nobody at the company had ever documented an answer to. One is a
> freshness problem, the other is an abstention problem, and a single accuracy number
> hides both.

#### "What's the largest-scope problem you've personally driven?"

> The questionnaire assistant, and I'd measure scope by what was attached to it rather
> than by headcount.
>
> It was a product with an entire lifecycle under it — requirements from customers and
> their lawyers, the full RAG architecture, production operation, a review queue, a
> pricing decision I influenced, and a partner program. And the output carried real
> exposure: these answers go to our customers' customers' auditors, so being wrong
> isn't an inconvenience, it's a legal problem for someone who trusted us.
>
> There was no one else it belonged to at any stage. Every failure of it was mine to
> diagnose, including the failure to sell.
>
> 60% less preparation effort, 85% of answers accepted on the customer's own click, and
> a partnership with a compliance platform that ended up reciprocal — they use our
> product, we use theirs.

#### "Tell me about a decision you made without enough information."

> The requirements for the questionnaire assistant.
>
> What I was given was that the answers had to be high quality and satisfy the
> customer. That's an adjective. And when I went to the customers, they couldn't define
> it either — they knew a bad answer when they saw one and couldn't tell me the rule in
> advance. There was no spec available to me, and I had to commit to an architecture
> anyway.
>
> So I stopped asking what quality meant and asked what they cared about most. Same
> answer every time: is it correct, and if this gets challenged, how do I defend it?
> Nobody used the word traceability. Turning "defend" into something buildable was my
> call, and I made it without being able to verify it first.
>
> I committed to four properties: grounded in the knowledge base, not the model's
> general knowledge; every answer traceable to its source record; no assumption when
> the knowledge base has nothing — surface it as unknown and hand off to a person; and
> human review in the loop so every miss returns as signal.
>
> That turned out to be the right bet, and not for the reason I expected. Traceability
> wasn't a quality feature. It was the thing that made customers willing to buy at all.

### Growth and self-direction

#### ★ "Tell me about difficult feedback you received. What did you do with it?"

> My manager at Meta told me I didn't give constructive feedback. I'd see a problem in
> how someone was working, or a collaboration heading the wrong way, and I'd work
> around it instead of saying it. And he framed it as a leadership weakness, not a
> style preference.
>
> My honest reaction was that it wasn't really my job. My view then was that if you're
> strong technically, people skills are a nice-to-have. I'd deliver, and the rest would
> sort itself out.
>
> What changed my mind wasn't the feedback itself. It was the following year. Work kept
> getting harder for reasons that had nothing to do with the technical problem —
> friction with people I needed, and the same issues coming up again and again because
> I'd never named them the first time. All of it was the thing I'd been told about.
>
> So I worked on it deliberately. I say it now, close to when it happens instead of
> saving it, and I say it in a way that keeps the relationship intact — the subject is
> the work, not the person.
>
> Where I saw it pay off was the data-layer project. Several teams were pulling the
> same data through different APIs, the numbers disagreed, and the PM and I wanted
> different things. He wanted the minimum work that made the problem go away — ship the
> improvement, move on. That's a reasonable instinct under pressure, but it wasn't
> counting what it cost us to keep operating four separate APIs, and every quick win
> was adding to that.
>
> A year earlier I'd have worked around him and just built what I thought was right.
> Instead I raised it early and put it on the work rather than on him: here's the
> maintenance cost we're already carrying, here's what it looks like in a year. We ended
> up doing both — the quick fix so users weren't blocked, then the unified API. And
> after that we had a shared way of talking about that tradeoff, which is the part that
> actually lasted.
>
> Where I've come out is that this isn't optional for anyone who wants real impact. You
> can be the strongest engineer in the room and still be the bottleneck, because the
> work doesn't move on your code alone.

#### "Tell me about a time you had to learn something quickly to unblock yourself."

> Late 2023, joining Kaamel, I had two things new at once. SOC 2, which I knew nothing
> about, and building on LLMs, which barely existed as a discipline — there was no book
> to read.
>
> Normally I'd learn a domain by collecting material and working through it
> systematically. I didn't have that kind of time, so I learned by conversation with
> the model.
>
> The risk there isn't that the model is sometimes wrong. It's that when you're new you
> can't tell which parts are wrong — a novice can't grade the tutor. So I leaned on the
> checks that don't require expertise.
>
> The cheapest one is consistency: track what it told you earlier, because
> contradictions are free to spot without knowing the domain. The second is watching
> the premises rather than the answers. At one point it told me to fix a quality
> problem by switching between LLMs and comparing them — but we only had the OpenAI
> API, so switching was never an option, and the problem wasn't model-related anyway. I
> stated the constraint rather than just rejecting the suggestion. Constraints aren't
> context, they're the search space; until you state them it keeps optimizing somewhere
> you don't live. And third, anything load-bearing, I'd go find the published document
> and check that it echoed. For the domain calls I had a lawyer in our founding group.
>
> That got me competent enough to design and ship the retrieval path, in two domains
> I'd started from zero in. The rule I came out with is: use the model to find the
> questions, never to settle them.

#### "Describe a time you identified a problem nobody asked you to fix."

> The data discrepancies at Meta. Nobody had reported it, because every team's own path
> to the data worked fine from where they sat. The inconsistency only existed between
> them, and it wasn't anybody's job.
>
> I suspected something was off and went digging. Several teams each had their own way
> of fetching the same network data, and once I lined them up, the numbers didn't
> agree. So the first problem wasn't the fix — it was that nobody knew this existed.
>
> What I did was make it visible and get the right people in one room: the users
> consuming these numbers, and the owners of each API. I built the comparison first, so
> everyone was looking at the same thing — per dataset, per metric, every path against
> every other. That mattered, because the alternative conversation is about whose
> numbers are wrong, and that only asks people to defend themselves.
>
> What came out of that room was the users' ask: fix the existing APIs, they wanted
> their numbers to agree quickly. So I made the tradeoff explicit — the quick fix
> first so nobody was blocked, then the unified API after, as the real solution. Both
> got done, and it ended up on 100% of users.
>
> What I'd do differently: I should have built that comparison in week one. I spent the
> early part of the project in meetings that a week of data would have ended.

#### "How do you stay current?"

> Two halves. The reading half is unremarkable — papers, releases, what the labs
> publish. The part that's actually changed how I work is the method.
>
> I use the model as the way in. On something new I'll have a conversation with it to
> find the ten percent that's load-bearing, then go verify that properly against the
> published source. Studying systematically means studying uniformly, because at the
> start you can't tell what matters. A conversation finds what matters, and then you
> check it. Use the model to find the questions, not to settle them.
>
> Day to day it's ChatGPT, Claude and Cursor across design, planning and coding. On a
> new initiative I hand over the requirement, have it break the product into
> components, then work through them one at a time. The output is a draft, not an
> answer.
>
> The single thing that improved my results most wasn't prompt wording. It's that as a
> conversation gets long, the model loses earlier context and the quality degrades
> without telling you. So I manage context deliberately instead of letting a thread
> sprawl.
>
> And the one that isn't reading at all: working next to people who solve the same
> problems differently. I've built one approach end to end, and I'd learn more from a
> team doing it at a different scale than from any amount of papers. That's a real part
> of why I'm here.

#### "Where should we *not* use AI?"

> Anywhere a confident wrong answer is both irreversible and asymmetric. That's the
> test I use — if this is wrong, can we take it back? If we can't, the model drafts and
> a person sends.
>
> In a support context that's refunds, policy commitments, anything legal or
> safety-related. Not because the model is bad at them; it might be fine most of the
> time. It's that the cost of the tail case isn't symmetric with the benefit of
> automating the common one.
>
> That's why human review on the questionnaire assistant was mandatory rather than a
> setting. Those answers go to auditors.
>
> And the related rule: when retrieval comes back weak, the system should say it
> doesn't know rather than produce something plausible. Abstention is a feature. A
> confident answer with nothing behind it is the worst output the system can give you,
> because it's the one nobody checks.

### On-call and operational reality

#### "This role is 24/7 on-call by rotation, including weekends. Are you comfortable with that?"

> Yes. I did 24/7 rotation when I was at Meta, so it isn't an unknown I'd be agreeing
> to in the abstract. It's normal to me.

#### "Tell me about a time you handled a production incident."

> AWS us-east-1, October 2025. It was down about fourteen and a half hours. We ran
> single-region, multi-AZ, in that region — small company, no follow-the-sun rotation.
>
> A customer told us they couldn't log in. A customer, not our monitoring. That's the
> first thing I'd say about it.
>
> When I looked, the logs were all on fire — SSO, the AI gateway, the database, all
> erroring. That's the real decision point, because with three things failing the
> temptation is to chase all three. I anchored on the one a user had actually reported.
> The other two were hypotheses; login was confirmed impact. So I deferred them
> deliberately.
>
> Login was failing because it couldn't fetch a token from AWS Secrets Manager. We were
> multi-AZ, but the outage took the whole region — multi-AZ bought us nothing. AWS had
> acknowledged it with no ETA, so instead of waiting I asked what we had outside the
> region. There was a Secrets Manager copy in us-west-1, not actively in use, and the
> token was already there. I cut login over and users could get in.
>
> I'll be straight about that part: the token being there was luck. Looking outside the
> region wasn't.
>
> Then the second wave. People could log in, but the gateway and the database were
> still degraded — over half our capacity, and no cross-region copy of either. There
> was nothing left to engineer. It wasn't a hard down; requests were succeeding
> intermittently. So the job changed, and I worked with colleagues to tell customers
> what was happening, that full recovery depended on AWS rather than on us, and that
> retrying genuinely did work some of the time.
>
> Login came back while the region was still down, and the rest rode it out. Two things
> I took from it. Multi-AZ doesn't protect you against a regional failure. And when
> everything is erroring at once, start where a user told you it hurts — logs tell you
> what's unhappy, a user tells you what's broken.

#### "How do you handle the pressure of an outage with partners waiting?"

> The pressure mostly comes from not being able to tell people anything, so I treat
> communication as part of the work rather than something that happens afterwards.
>
> In the AWS outage the hardest part wasn't the first hour. It was the second wave —
> users could log in again, but the gateway and the database were still degraded, we
> had no cross-region copy of either, and there was genuinely nothing left for me to
> engineer. That's uncomfortable, because the instinct is to keep typing so it looks
> like you're doing something. Knowing when to stop debugging is the actual skill
> there.
>
> So I switched to communication, and I got customers three specific things: what was
> broken, that full recovery depended on AWS and not on us, and that retrying did work
> some of the time — which was useful to them rather than just reassuring.
>
> The way I handle the pressure itself is to keep it factual and frequent. People can
> tolerate a long outage. What they can't tolerate is silence, or a recovery estimate
> you invented.

#### "What's your approach to reducing on-call load over time?"

> Two things, and they're really the same idea: make problems visible before a person
> has to notice them.
>
> The direct one from that outage — a customer knew before our monitoring did. That's
> the real failure, more than the outage itself. The fix is external synthetic
> monitoring, a login probe running from outside the region. If your monitoring lives
> in the region that's down, it goes down with you.
>
> The other is from Meta. The discrepancy dashboard I built for the data layer was the
> same move. Those numbers had been disagreeing for a long time and nobody knew,
> because nothing was watching for it. Once the comparison ran continuously, the
> problem surfaced itself instead of waiting for someone to trip over it.
>
> Beyond that it's the ordinary work — every page should be actionable or deleted, and
> anything that's woken someone up twice deserves a runbook or a fix. But most on-call
> load I've seen comes from finding out late, not from the alerts themselves.

## Delivery mechanics

- **Answer the question that was asked.** The most common failure is retrofitting
  a prepared story onto a question it doesn't fit. If it doesn't fit, take three
  seconds and pick another; that pause costs nothing.
- **Lead with the headline.** "We launched it and customers wouldn't buy it" —
  then back up and fill in. Don't build to a reveal.
- **Say "I," not "we."** This round asks for *your* specific role and
  contribution, in those words. Use "we" for context and "I" for actions.
- **Two to three minutes per answer.** Then stop and let them probe. Over-talking
  crowds out the follow-ups, and the follow-ups are where you score.
- **State a number once, flatly, and move on.** 60%, 85%, 100%, $7k — inflating any
  of them invites a follow-up you can't survive.
- **It's fine to think.** "Let me pick the best example" is a complete sentence.
- **Keep the one-page cheat sheet** of story handles visible off-camera. Handles
  only — reading full answers is audible. It's at the end of the
  [story bank](<./Story Bank - Rounds 4 & 5.md#one-page-cheat-sheet>).

## Questions to ask the hiring manager

The guide lists this under General Tips for every round. Have 3–4 per interviewer
and make them different — the loop compares notes.

- How is the team's scope split between reactive partner escalations and proactive
  tooling? Where do you want that ratio in a year?
- What does the on-call rotation actually look like day to day, and what's the
  team doing to reduce its load?
- What separates someone who does well in this role from someone who struggles?
- Business Agent went global recently — what's changed for the support org as
  volume scaled?
- What's the biggest gap on the team right now that you're hoping this hire fills?

Avoid asking anything answerable from the job posting.
